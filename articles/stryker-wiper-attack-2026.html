Stryker Wiper Deep-Dive: Handala's Abuse of Microsoft Intune to Remote-Wipe 80,000 Devices

Harish G • March 31, 2026 • 25 min read

Executive Summary

On March 11, 2026, the cybersecurity landscape witnessed a significant event: a mass remote-wipe campaign targeting global enterprises. Approximately 80,000 devices across 79 countries were rendered inoperable through the exploitation of Microsoft Intune’s administrative capabilities. Attributed to the Iran-linked group "Handala," the attack represents a sophisticated example of "Living-off-the-Cloud" (LotC) tactics. By bypassing traditional security perimeters through session hijacking and abusing legitimate device management features, the attackers demonstrated the vulnerability of centralized cloud management when not sufficiently hardened. This technical deep-dive analyzes the attack sequence and provides a detailed strategy for organizational resilience.

I’ve been closely tracking the Stryker Wiper incident for three weeks, following it since the first reports emerged on March 11. By synthesizing information from verified news reports, active discussions in developer and security communities like r/netsec and r/cybersecurity, official Microsoft security advisories, and public technical analysis from incident responders, a clear picture of the event has developed. This analysis moves beyond the headlines to examine the technical mechanics of the attack and, crucially, the specific architectural changes required to prevent a recurrence.

The significance of this event lies not in the use of novel malware—of which there was none—but in the clinical abuse of legitimate enterprise tools. This is the new reality of cloud-native threats: the tools used to manage our environments are being turned into the primary vectors for their destruction.

1. The Timeline: Sequence of a Cloud-Native Attack

Reconstructing the event timeline reveals a phased approach that prioritized stealth and reconnaissance before the final execution of the wipe commands. The attackers spent several days inside the targeted environments before taking any destructive action.

Phase 1: Session Hijacking and Initial Access (March 5 - March 10)

Access was not gained through brute force but through sophisticated session hijacking. By leveraging Adversary-in-the-Middle (AitM) phishing and infostealer malware, the Handala group captured authenticated session cookies from administrative accounts. This allowed them to reuse established, trusted sessions that had already satisfied Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) requirements, effectively bypassing security policies meant to verify identity.

Phase 2: Tenant Reconnaissance (March 10)

Once inside the Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure AD) environment, the attackers used the compromised Global Administrator credentials to enumerate the device inventory in Microsoft Intune. Forensic logs show systematic querying of the Microsoft Graph API to identify high-value targets and map out the device management landscape. This was a targeted mapping exercise intended to maximize the impact of the subsequent phase.

Phase 3: Execution of the Mass Wipe (March 11, 04:00 UTC)

The destructive phase was automated and rapid. Utilizing scripts to interact directly with the Graph API, the attackers issued mass wipe commands. Within a four-hour window, corporate workstations, laptops, and mobile devices across the globe began the Windows "Resetting this PC" process. Because the management plane itself was the source of the command, endpoint protection platforms (EPP) and EDRs did not intervene, treating the command as a legitimate administrative action.

2. Technical Analysis: The "Living-off-the-Cloud" Methodology

The defining characteristic of the Stryker event was the complete absence of traditional malware. The attackers did not need to bypass antivirus or endpoint detection and response (EDR) systems on individual devices because they were issuing commands through the management layer that these systems are designed to trust.

Abuse of the Graph API and Intune Commands

The core of the attack involved programmatic calls to the Microsoft Graph API, specifically targeting the managedDevices/wipe and managedDevices/retire endpoints. These are standard features used by IT departments to clear data from lost or stolen devices or to decommission old hardware. By automating these calls at scale, the attackers turned a management feature into a weapon for mass data destruction.

Specifically, the attackers utilized the flag to "Wipe device, and continue to wipe even if device loses power." This setting ensures that the reset process is persistent; if a user attempts to interrupt the process by powering down the machine, it simply resumes once the device is restarted, making it exceptionally difficult to halt once initiated.

The Failure of Traditional MFA

This event highlights a critical weakness in many current security implementations: the reliance on session-based MFA that can be bypassed through ticket/cookie theft. Because the Handala group captured the session cookies rather than the credentials themselves, the Entra ID platform treated the attacker's traffic as a continuation of a previously verified, legitimate session. This underscores the need for phishing-resistant authentication methods that bind a session to a specific physical device.

Operational Comparison: Traditional ransomware (which encrypts files for ransom) versus the cloud-native wiper approach (which abuses management tools for direct destruction).

3. Victimology and Targeted Sectors

While the attack was global, its impact was not uniform. Handala appears to have prioritized sectors where a mass wipe would cause significant operational disruption, such as finance, logistics, and large-scale manufacturing. These industries depend heavily on centralized device management for distributed workforces.

A notable observation in several research reports is that patient-facing medical equipment remained largely unaffected. This is likely due to the separate management networks often used for critical healthcare IoT, which are frequently isolated from the primary corporate Intune tenant. This isolation provided an unintended layer of protection, emphasizing the value of network and management segmentation.

4. Organizational Resilience: A Detailed Hardening Strategy

The Stryker incident necessitates a re-evaluation of how organizations secure their cloud management planes. The following sections provide a technical roadmap for hardening Microsoft Entra ID and Intune environments against similar LotC attacks. This section constitutes the core of our technical recommendations for security architects.

4.1. Implementing Phishing-Resistant Authentication

Standard MFA (SMS, Voice, or Push notifications) is no longer sufficient for administrative accounts. The absolute priority for any organization using Microsoft 365 is to transition all high-privilege users to phishing-resistant MFA.

Configuring Conditional Access for Phishing-Resistance:
To enforce this, you should create or modify a Conditional Access (CA) policy in Microsoft Entra Admin Center:

  1. Assignment: Target binary "Global Administrator" and other high-privilege roles.
  2. Target Resources: Apply to "All Cloud Apps" or specifically to "Microsoft Intune" and "Microsoft Azure Management".
  3. Access Controls: Under "Grant," select "Require authentication strength."
  4. Authentication Strength: Choose "Phishing-resistant MFA." This strictly limits access to methods like FIDO2 security keys (e.g., Yubikey) or Windows Hello for Business, which bind the authentication to the physical hardware and cannot be captured via AitM phishing.
Hardening Access: Configuring Microsoft Entra ID Conditional Access to mandate phishing-resistant MFA for administrative roles.

4.2. Eliminating Standing Privileges via PIM

No administrator should have permanent Global Admin rights. The "Stryker" attack succeeded because the compromised sessions had persistent, high-level access. Privileged Identity Management (PIM) must be used to ensure that administrative rights are only available when needed and only after additional verification.

Configuring PIM with Multi-Party Approval:
For destructive or sensitive roles like "Intune Administrator," implement a "Just-In-Time" (JIT) access workflow:

  1. Activation Requirement: Require MFA for activation (this must be phishing-resistant).
  2. Approval Requirement: Require approval for activation. Designate at least two "Approvers" (e.g., a Security Manager and a Lead Infrastructure Architect).
  3. Activation Duration: Limit the activation to a short window (e.g., 2-4 hours).
  4. Justification: Require a ticket number and business justification for the activation.

4.3. Custom RBAC Roles: The Principle of Least Privilege

The standard "Intune Administrator" role is often too broad for daily operational tasks. Organizations should decompose this role into specific, lower-privilege custom roles to limit the potential blast radius of a single compromised session.

Creating a "Helpdesk - Non-Destructive" Role in Intune:
By creating a custom role, you can allow support staff to perform their jobs without the ability to trigger a mass wipe.

  1. Navigate to Tenant Administration > roles > All roles > Create.
  2. Permissions: Grant permissions for "Remote tasks" like Remote lock, Reset password, and Sync.
  3. Restriction: Do not grant permissions for Wipe, Retire, or Delete.
  4. Assignment: Assign this role to your primary support staff via Entra ID Groups. Reserve full Wipe capability for a separate, highly restricted group behind PIM.
Management Plane Hardening: Implementing a tiered administration model with JIT access and decomposed RBAC roles to minimize systemic risk.

4.4. Proactive Detection and Monitoring with KQL

Real-time detection is essential to catch an attack in its early stages. Security teams should implement automated alerting in Microsoft Sentinel (or their SIEM of choice) to monitor for anomalous administrative actions.

KQL Query: Detection of Bulk Remote Wipe Actions
This query alerts when multiple wipe commands are issued by a single account within a short timeframe, which is a key indicator of automated attack scripts.

Alert Rule: Bulk Intune Device Wipe Category: Administrative Abuse 
// Detects more than 10 'Wipe managed device' commands from a single user in 15 minutes.
AuditLogs
| where OperationName == "Wipe managed device" or OperationName == "Retire managed device"
| where Result == "success"
| summarize Count = count(), DeviceNames = make_set(TargetResources[0].displayName) by InitiatedBy.user.userPrincipalName, bin(TimeGenerated, 15m)
| where Count > 10
| project TimeGenerated, InitiatedBy_user_userPrincipalName, Count, DeviceNames
| extend AccountCustomEntity = InitiatedBy_user_userPrincipalName

4.5. Configuration-as-Code and Recovery Workflows

Resilience is not just about prevention; it is about the speed of recovery. Organizations must have the ability to rebuild their management environment from a known good state.

Using Microsoft365DSC for Configuration Backup:
Microsoft365DSC allows you to treat your M365 tenant configuration as code. You can "export" your entire Intune configuration into a series of DSC scripts.

  • Schedule Regular Exports: Run a weekly export of your tenant configuration.
  • Store in Secure Repository: Store these scripts in a version-controlled repository outside your M365 environment.
  • Automated Recovery: If your tenant's configuration is tampered with, you can "push" the known-good configuration back to the tenant, restoring your security posture in minutes.

5. Conclusion: The Shift to Management Plane Security

The Stryker Wiper incident represents an evolution in threat activity where the management plane itself is the target. By technical analysis of the Handala group's methods, it is clear that traditional endpoint security is insufficient when the administrative controls themselves are compromised. Moving forward, organizations must prioritize the security of their identity and management platforms with the same intensity they apply to their data and network perimeters. Transitioning to phishing-resistant MFA, eliminating standing privileges via PIM, and adopting configuration-as-code are now the essential foundations of enterprise resilience in a cloud-first world.
