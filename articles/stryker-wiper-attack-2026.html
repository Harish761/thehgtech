I’ve Been Tracking the Stryker Wiper Attack for Three Weeks — Here’s Exactly What Happened, How the Attackers Did It, and What Every Organisation Must Do Now

Harish G • March 31, 2026 • 25 min read

Executive Summary

On March 11, 2026, a coordinated "cloud-native" wiper attack targeted global enterprises, resulting in the remote wiping of over 80,000 devices via Microsoft Intune. Attributed to the Iran-linked "Handala" group, the attack relied entirely on the abuse of legitimate administrative features rather than traditional malware. This technical analysis explores the "Living-off-the-Cloud" (LotC) techniques used, the bypass of MFA through session hijacking, and provides a comprehensive resilience framework for Microsoft Entra ID and Intune environments.

I’ve been closely tracking this incident since March 11 by following verified news reports, Reddit threads in r/netsec and r/cybersecurity, official Microsoft advisories, and public technical analysis from security researchers globally. What we are witnessing is not just another data breach; it is a fundamental shift in how state-sponsored hacktivists define "impact" in the cloud era.

1. The Timeline: 48 Hours of Operational Decapitation

The Stryker Wiper campaign didn't start with a bang, but with a silent series of credential thefts. Based on multiple incident reports and forensic reconstructions, the timeline followed a clinical progression:

Phase 1: Session Hijacking (March 5 - March 10)

Before the first wipe command was ever sent, the Handala group spent days harvesting administrative sessions. Rather than attempting to crack passwords, they leveraged AitM (Adversary-in-the-Middle) phishing and infostealer malware to capture authenticated session cookies. This allowed them to bypass Conditional Access policies that required MFA, as they were effectively "riding" on an already established, trusted session of a Global Administrator.

Phase 2: The Intune Reconnaissance (March 10)

Logs from multiple affected tenants show that on March 10, compromised accounts were used to enumerate Intune device lists. Attackers were not looking for data to steal; they were mapping the scope of the "Remote Wipe" and "Factory Reset" capabilities across corporate workstations and mobile devices.

Phase 3: The Mass Wipe (March 11, 04:00 UTC)

The attackers executed a series of automated scripts hitting the Graph API. Within a 4-hour window, mass wipe commands were issued to approximately 80,000 devices across 79 countries. By the time IT teams woke up in London and New York, their fleets were already displaying the Windows "Resetting this PC" screen.

Attack Reconstruction: Visual timeline from initial session theft to mass remote wipe execution.

2. Technical Breakdown: The "Living-off-the-Cloud" Tactic

The most striking aspect of the Stryker attack is the total absence of traditional malware. There was no `.exe`, no PowerShell obfuscation, and no C2 beaconing in the traditional sense. The "malware" was simply the Microsoft Graph API.

Abusing the 'Remote Wipe' Command

The core of the attack was the abuse of the `managedDevices/wipe` and `managedDevices/retire` commands in Microsoft Intune. By programmatically iterating through every device ID in a tenant, the attackers could issue a "Factory Reset" command that effectively turns a $2,000 corporate laptop into a paperweight until it can be re-imaged.

Critically, the attackers frequently selected the option to "Wipe device, and continue to wipe even if device loses power." This flag ensured that even if a user noticed the reset starting and pulled the plug, the process would resume the moment power was restored, guaranteeing data destruction.

The MFA Bypass Gap

Many organisations believed they were safe because they had MFA enabled for all admins. However, the Stryker attack proved that MFA is not a silver bullet against session theft. If an admin logs in from a trusted device and their cookie is stolen, that cookie is still valid for hours—sometimes days—depending on the Token Lifetime policies. Handala simply imported these cookies into a hardened browser and proceeded as the legitimate user.

Operational Difference: Traditional ransomware (locking files) vs. Modern Device Management abuse (remote wipe commands).

3. Victimology: Why Hospitals Were Spared But Banks Weren't

Initial reports suggested a random spray-and-pray attack, but deeper analysis reveals a targeted approach. While 80,000 devices were hit, patient-facing medical devices were notably unaffected. This is likely due to the fact that medical IoT devices are rarely managed via Intune or Entra ID in the same way corporate laptops are.

Conversely, financial institutions and logistics firms saw the highest impact. These organisations rely heavily on Intune for "Zero Touch Deployment" of workstations. By hitting the management layer, Handala didn't just delete files; they destroyed the very mechanism used to fix the systems.

4. Forensic Artifacts and Detection

For organisations currently performing "clean-room" audits of their Entra ID environment, specific artifacts have emerged as "smoking guns" of the Stryker activity:

  • AuditLogs: Look for `Wipe managed device` operations originating from unexpected IP addresses or through "Managed Search" API calls.
  • Sign-in Logs: Anomalous browser strings. Attackers used specific versions of "Hardened Firefox" and "Go-Login" browsers to simulate legitimate sessions while avoiding detection by generic bot-blockers.
  • Graph API Calls: Mass calls to `https://graph.microsoft.com/beta/deviceManagement/managedDevices` within short timeframes.

5. How to Protect Your Organisation: The Resilience Framework

This is the most critical section of this analysis. If your current security posture assumes that "MFA + Global Admin = Secure," the Stryker attack has proven you wrong. We must move toward a model of Zero Standing Privileges and Multi-Party Authorization.

5.1. Hardening Conditional Access (CA)

Generic CA policies are no longer enough. You must implement Phishing-Resistant MFA. If you are still using SMS, Voice, or Push Notifications for Global Admins, you are vulnerable to the same session hijacking used in Stryker.

Recommendation: Create a CA policy that mandates FIDO2 Security Keys (e.g., YubiKey) or Windows Hello for Business for all administrative paths. This ensures that even if a session cookie is stolen, the attacker cannot re-authenticate or perform high-sensitivity actions without physical hardware possession.

Resilience Blueprint: Implementing Privileged Identity Management (PIM) and Multi-Party Authorization (MPA) for destructive commands.

5.2. Implementing "Non-Destructive" Custom RBAC Roles

Why does your L1 helpdesk need the ability to wipe 10,000 devices? In most organisations, the default "Intune Administrator" role is vastly over-privileged.

Step-by-step:

  1. Navigate to Intune > Tenant Administration > Roles.
  2. Create a Custom Role named "Helpdesk Support - No Wipe".
  3. In permissions, grant `Remote tasks` for everything except Wipe and Retire.
  4. Assign this to your primary support staff via Entra ID Groups.
  5. Reserve the full Wipe capability for a separate, highly restricted group that requires PIM (Privileged Identity Management) activation WITH an approval workflow.

Hardening CA Policies: Mandating phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2) for all administrative paths in Microsoft Entra ID.

5.3. Production-Ready KQL for Sentinel/Defender

You cannot wait for users to call the helpdesk. You must alert on mass wipe attempts in real-time. Use the following KQL query in Microsoft Sentinel to detect potential Stryker-like activity:

Detection: Mass Remote Wipe Activity Category: Threat Detection 
// Detect more than 5 Remote Wipe commands from a single user in 10 minutes
AuditLogs
| where OperationName has "Wipe managed device" or OperationName has "Retire managed device"
| where Result == "success"
| summarize count(), DeviceList = make_set(TargetResources[0].displayName) by InitiatedBy.user.userPrincipalName, bin(TimeGenerated, 10m)
| where count_ > 5
| project TimeGenerated, InitiatedBy_user_userPrincipalName, count_, DeviceList
| extend AccountCustomEntity = InitiatedBy_user_userPrincipalName

5.4. Backup via Microsoft365DSC (Configuration-as-Code)

Most organisations backup their data, but almost none backup their Intune Configuration. If an attacker deletes your Autopilot profiles or CA policies, how long would it take you to rebuild from memory?

Start using Microsoft365DSC (Digital Storage Cloud). This allows you to export your entire Intune configuration as a PowerShell script. Store this in a private GitHub repository. If your tenant is ever "wiped" at the config level, you can redeploy your entire security posture in minutes.

6. Conclusion: The Paradigm of Cloud Resilience

The Stryker Wiper attack is a painful reminder that the cloud provides as much leverage to defenders as it does to attackers. When we centralise management into Intune, we gain efficiency, but we also create a "God Mode" that, once compromised, can destroy an entire global fleet in an afternoon.

True resilience in 2026 isn't just about better firewalls; it’s about limiting the blast radius of your own administrative features. Disable what you don't use, restrict who can initiate destruction, and always — always — have your configuration stored as code.
