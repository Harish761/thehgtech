The False Positive Paradox: Why EDRs Flag Genuine Processes

Technical analysis of why EDRs flag genuine developer tools and vulnerability scanners, and how SOC teams should react without panic.

May 25, 2026 Harish G 10 min read
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
  • BLUF: As EDR and AV tools shift from signature-based detection to aggressive behavioral heuristics, false positives are no longer rare anomalies.
  • Recent high-profile incidents involving CrowdStrike, Microsoft Defender, and Nessus highlight a growing operational challenge for Security Operations Centers (SOCs).
  • Confidence Level: Confirmed.
  • TLP: CLEAR.

This analysis is based on recent telemetry and vendor disclosures as of May 2026; details may evolve.

It’s 3:00 AM on a Saturday. PagerDuty triggers a P1 critical alert: multiple endpoints across your engineering environment are experiencing process terminations and heuristic blocks. The culprit? A vulnerability scanner (like Tenable Nessus) or a routine developer workflow (git push). The SOC drops everything, suspecting a massive supply chain breach. Hours later, the dust settles—it was a false positive.

False positives from tools like CrowdStrike, Defender, and SentinelOne are not bugs—they are often the tools working exactly as designed. Instead of complaining about EDR noise, SOCs need to understand why the heuristic engines behave this way and how to respond systematically instead of reacting with panic.

1. The Incidents: When Defenders Clash with Defense

The last six months have seen several high-profile incidents where major EDR/AV solutions aggressively flagged genuine processes, causing massive alert fatigue.

  • CrowdStrike vs. Tenable Nessus (Late May 2026): CrowdStrike Falcon actively blocked and terminated legitimate Nessus PowerShell scripts checking for the "Miniplasma" zero-day. Vulnerability scans were killed mid-flight, triggering mass alerts.
  • CrowdStrike vs. GitHub Assets (March 2026): A widespread false positive where routine DNS resolutions to release-assets.githubusercontent.com triggered critical Indicator of Compromise (IOC) alerts, briefly halting CI/CD pipelines as SOC teams scrambled to isolate developer machines.
  • Microsoft Defender vs. DigiCert (Early May 2026): In response to a DigiCert security incident, Microsoft pushed an aggressive signature update aiming to catch compromised certificates. Instead, Defender flagged legitimate DigiCert root certificates as Trojan:Win32/Cerdigent.A!dha, removing them from the Windows trust store and breaking corporate applications.

2. Under the Hood: Why Heuristic Engines Overreact

Security tools are not broken; they are hyper-vigilant.

  • Behavioral Parity (Scanner vs. Attacker): When Nessus scans a network for a vulnerability, it often executes the exact same WMI queries, PowerShell commands, and port sweeps that a threat actor or ransomware strain would use during lateral movement. To a behavioral engine, a match is a match.
  • Supply Chain Hyper-Sensitivity: In a post-SolarWinds and post-XZ Utils world, attackers love hiding in developer environments. Consequently, EDRs now aggressively monitor developer tools like Git, compilers, and CI/CD runners.
  • The "Catch Rate" Trade-off During Zero-Days: When a massive vulnerability is announced, EDR vendors immediately dial up their sensitivity rules. They intentionally prioritize a high "catch rate" over the operational inconvenience of a false positive.
  • Packed & Obfuscated Binaries: Many legitimate developer tools use packers to compress their binaries. EDRs inherently distrust packed executables because malware authors use the exact same techniques to evade detection.

Example Detection Logic

Why Nessus triggers EDR behaviors during a scan:

# Pseudo-logic of an EDR Behavioral Rule
if (Process.Name == "powershell.exe" and 
    Process.CommandLine.Contains("-EncodedCommand") and 
    ParentProcess.IsUnrecognizedNetworkAgent()):
    # Attacker lateral movement... OR just Nessus scanning remotely.
    Trigger.CriticalAlert("Suspected Remote Code Execution via PowerShell")
    Action.TerminateProcessHierarchy()

3. The Anatomy of SOC Panic

What happens operationally and psychologically when SOCs react poorly to these alerts?

  • Alert Fatigue & The "Boy Who Cried Wolf": When analysts spend 8 hours clearing 500 benign alerts generated by a routine Nessus scan or a Git deployment, they become desensitized. If a real PowerShell threat hides within that noise, the fatigued analyst is likely to bulk-close it.
  • Business Interruption: Uncoordinated, panic-driven responses—such as instantly isolating developer laptops off the network because a GitHub domain triggered an alert—disrupt engineering velocity and damage trust.
  • The Danger of Knee-Jerk Exclusions: In the heat of a false-positive storm, panicked IT admins often implement the most dangerous "fix": blanket whitelisting. Excluding an entire directory like C:\Program Files\Tenable\ creates massive, unmonitored blind spots for attackers to leverage.

4. The Strategic Playbook: Taming the Noise

Actionable, step-by-step advice for security operations teams to handle aggressive EDRs without compromising security.

THE RESPONSE PLAYBOOK:
  1. Contextualize Before Escalating: Check the telemetry. Did git.exe launch from a known developer machine during normal hours? Context is the antidote to panic.
  2. Mutual Security Tool Trust: Security tools will attack each other if not properly introduced. Add your verified vulnerability scanners (like Nessus) to your primary AV/EDR's exclusion scanning list so some routine alerts can be suppressed. (Note: Reach out to your security vendors to get the exact process information.)
  3. Surgical Exclusions, Not Blanket Allow-Lists: Never exclude whole folders. Use highly targeted parameters (e.g., specific file hashes, strict command-line argument matches).
  4. Verify Against Vendor Intelligence: Check vendor portals immediately. High-profile false positives are usually acknowledged within 1–2 hours.
  5. Cross-Team Communication: Maintain a direct channel between the SOC, Vulnerability Management, and Engineering to anticipate scan noise.

Conclusion: EDRs are hyper-vigilant by necessity. A false positive on a vulnerable process isn't a failure—it is proof that the behavioral engine is watching the right things. SOCs must treat these alerts not as "tool failures" but as expected operational friction. By establishing mutual tool trust, building robust triage processes, and communicating with engineering, teams can handle the noise without losing their edge.
