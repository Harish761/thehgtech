TCS Digital Tracking Controversy: DEM vs Employee Surveillance

Harish G August 24, 2026 14 min read

Executive Summary

Recent allegations that Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) deployed individual tracking software sparked significant privacy concerns. TCS officially denied these claims, stating the tools are strictly for macro-level Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM) and cybersecurity telemetry. This controversy highlights the friction between enterprise security observability and modern privacy expectations under frameworks like the DPDP Act and ISO 27701.

Key Findings

  • Verified: TCS deployed infrastructure monitoring tooling to manage endpoints and network health.
  • Refuted: TCS officially denies tracking individual application usage or keystrokes for employee surveillance.
  • The Technology: DEM (Digital Experience Monitoring) inherently collects granular endpoint telemetry, creating friction if data minimization policies are unclear.
  • Compliance Impact: Endpoint monitoring deployments must strictly align with DPDP Act legitimate purpose provisions and ISO 27001 Annex A.12.4.

What Happened: The Tracking Allegations

Media Reports In August 2026, a controversy erupted across professional networking platforms following reports from Moneycontrol and other financial outlets indicating that Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) had activated new "digital tracking" mechanisms across its nearly 600,000-strong global workforce.

The initial reports, heavily circulated on LinkedIn, cited anonymous sources claiming that the deployed software monitored individual laptop activity. Allegations suggested the software tracked specific application usage, the exact time spent on those applications, and active window states. These capabilities sparked immediate fears of micromanagement, "bossware" surveillance, and severe privacy overreach.

The reaction highlighted a growing tension in remote and hybrid work models: the workforce's expectation of privacy versus the enterprise's mandate to secure intellectual property and monitor remote assets.

The Official Response: Macro vs. Micro

TCS Statement Facing significant public and internal pushback, TCS issued categorical denials of the surveillance claims. The company labeled the reports of individual tracking as "baseless and inaccurate."

According to the official corporate response, the software in question is not designed for, nor used for, employee surveillance. TCS clarified that the deployment is a Digital User Experience Monitoring (DUEM) solution. Its stated objectives are purely operational and security-focused:

  • Monitoring macro-level network performance.
  • Ensuring corporate network security and availability.
  • Proactively identifying hardware or software bottlenecks affecting employee productivity.

TheHGTech Analysis The distinction TCS draws is critical. The company asserts that the telemetry is aggregated and utilized for IT operations and threat detection, rather than weaponized by HR or management to monitor individual employee bathroom breaks or idle time. However, this distinction relies on internal policy and access controls, which are invisible to the end user—hence the ensuing controversy.

What is Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM)?

To understand the controversy, we must separate the technology from the policy. Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM)—also known as Digital Employee Experience (DEX) monitoring—is a mature category of enterprise IT software recognized by Gartner and Forrester. Prominent vendors in this space include Zscaler (ZDX), Nexthink, Riverbed, and ThousandEyes.

DEM platforms are designed to solve a massive operational challenge: when a remote employee complains that "the VPN is slow" or "Teams is dropping calls," IT needs to know exactly where the failure is occurring.

How DEM Actually Works

DEM agents run locally on the endpoint (the employee's laptop) with SYSTEM or root privileges. They continuously map the network path from the device to critical SaaS applications (like Microsoft 365 or Salesforce). To do this, they collect:

  • CPU, memory, and disk I/O metrics.
  • Wi-Fi signal strength and local router metrics.
  • DNS resolution times and HTTP latency.
  • Application crash logs and resource consumption (e.g., "Chrome.exe is using 4GB of RAM").

While this telemetry is invaluable for identifying that an employee's slow application is due to a failing local ISP rather than a corporate firewall, the agent undeniably collects highly granular data about what the laptop is doing at all times.

The Line Between Monitoring and Surveillance

The core of the TCS controversy—and the challenge for the broader industry—is that the technical capabilities of DEM platforms and "bossware" (surveillance software) heavily overlap. The difference lies entirely in configuration, data retention, and access control policies.

Consider the following technical comparison of capabilities natively present in most modern endpoint agents:

Telemetry Data Point Legitimate Security / IT Use Case The Surveillance (Bossware) Abuse Case
Active Application Logs Identifying if unapproved shadow IT (e.g., unauthorized VPNs) is running, or finding memory leaks in corporate apps. Generating reports on whether an employee is spending time on Netflix vs. Visual Studio.
Network Traffic Destinations Detecting C2 (Command and Control) beacons or data exfiltration to known malicious domains. Monitoring which news sites or job boards an employee visits during business hours.
Keystroke / Mouse Activity Zero legitimate IT operational use. Sometimes used in highly secure VDI for DLP. "Idle tracking." Docking pay or flagging employees who haven't moved their mouse in 10 minutes.
Screen Captures Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) might capture a frame during a malware execution event. Taking screenshots every 5 minutes to verify the employee is physically at their desk.

TheHGTech Analysis TCS explicitly denies utilizing the capabilities listed in the "Surveillance Abuse Case" column. For an enterprise of TCS's scale, managing 600,000 endpoints without DEM or EDR telemetry would be cybersecurity negligence. However, without transparent communication about what data is discarded, employees naturally assume the maximum capability of the software is being utilized against them.

Privacy, DPDP Act, and ISO Compliance

Enterprise monitoring does not exist in a vacuum. Organizations must navigate strict legal and compliance frameworks that dictate how endpoint telemetry is handled.

India's DPDP Act (2023) Implications

DPDP Act The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act of India imposes stringent requirements on data fiduciaries. While companies have legitimate grounds to monitor their assets, the law enforces the principle of Data Minimization. If an enterprise claims it needs network metrics for cybersecurity, it cannot legally harvest and retain personal web browsing history unless explicitly consented to or strictly required for employment purposes.

Under the DPDP Act, employees must be informed via a clear notice about the nature of the personal data being collected and the purpose of its processing. Obfuscating "idle tracking" under the guise of "network security" would be a direct violation of the Act's purpose limitation principles.

ISO 27001 and ISO 27701 (PIMS)

For organizations certified under ISO 27001 (Information Security) and ISO 27701 (Privacy Information Management):

  • ISO 27001 Control 8.16 (Monitoring activities): Mandates that networks and systems shall be monitored for anomalous behavior. Security telemetry is a compliance requirement.
  • ISO 27701 Clause 7.3: Requires the organization to limit the collection of PII (Personally Identifiable Information) to what is strictly necessary.

To satisfy both standards simultaneously, organizations must implement Privacy Enhancing Technologies (PETs) at the agent level—such as stripping URLs of query parameters, anonymizing hostnames, and utilizing Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) so that HR cannot view the raw endpoint telemetry intended for the Security Operations Center (SOC).

NIST Privacy Framework Considerations

In addition to ISO standards, organizations aligning with the NIST Privacy Framework (NIST PF) must consider the Data Processing Policies, Procedures, and Agreements (PO.P) function. The framework emphasizes that data processing actions—such as capturing telemetry from an employee's machine—must have a clear governance structure. Under NIST PF, deploying DEM without communicating the specific privacy risks and data minimization rules to the workforce constitutes a failure in the Communication (CM-P) category, leading to the exact trust deficit seen in this incident.

What We Know vs. What Remains Unknown

What is Verified

  • TCS deployed a Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM) solution to corporate assets.
  • Media reports cited anonymous internal sources claiming the tool was tracking granular application time.
  • TCS issued an official, on-the-record denial stating the tool is used exclusively for macro-level network and security telemetry.
  • Corporate monitoring of company-owned assets for security purposes is a standard, legally defensible industry practice.

What Remains Unknown

  • The specific software vendor (e.g., Zscaler, Nexthink) utilized by TCS was not publicly confirmed.
  • The exact data retention lifecycle for the collected endpoint telemetry.
  • The internal RBAC policies governing which TCS departments (IT vs. HR) have access to the aggregated DEM dashboards.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM)?

Digital Experience Monitoring (DEM) is a technology framework used by enterprises to track network performance, application latency, and endpoint health to ensure a seamless digital workflow for employees.

Did TCS install surveillance software to track individual employees?

TCS officially denied allegations of individual employee surveillance, stating that the tools deployed are strictly for macro-level network performance and cybersecurity monitoring, not tracking individual keystrokes or screens.

How does the DPDP Act impact enterprise monitoring?

Under India's DPDP Act, organizations must establish clear legitimate purpose, obtain consent (or rely on legitimate employment purposes), and ensure data minimization when monitoring digital activity on company-owned assets.

Conclusion

The controversy surrounding TCS's deployment of endpoint monitoring tools is symptomatic of a broader industry communication failure. Enterprises have an absolute requirement—mandated by security frameworks and compliance audits—to monitor their corporate networks and endpoints. Without Digital Experience Monitoring and Endpoint Detection, defending against modern cyber threats is impossible.

However, because the technical capabilities of security agents and surveillance "bossware" overlap, deployment without transparent internal communication breeds immediate distrust. Based on the verified statements, TCS is executing a standard IT operational rollout. Moving forward, the burden lies on enterprise security teams globally to architect these deployments with privacy-by-design, ensuring that telemetry collected for security cannot be repurposed for surveillance.
