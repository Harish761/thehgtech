The Context Crisis in Cybersecurity: Why Security Alerts Make Less Sense Than Ever

The cybersecurity industry increasingly rewards tool knowledge, framework knowledge, and alert handling skills while unintentionally reducing exposure to the infrastructure and operational knowledge required to properly understand those alerts.

Executive Summary (BLUF)

  • The Context Gap is the growing disconnect between an analyst's mastery of security frameworks (like MITRE ATT&CK) and their understanding of operational IT infrastructure.
  • Many SOC investigations become longer, noisier, and less accurate not because analysts lack security knowledge, but because security context has become separated from operational context.
  • A significant portion of critical alerts (brute force, data exfiltration, privilege escalation) are actually caused by completely normal, automated IT behaviors that mimic threat actor techniques.
  • As AI and autonomous agents take on more triage workloads, understanding underlying systems architecture—not just security dashboards—will become the most vital skill for the future analyst.

This analysis is based on real-world enterprise SOC observations and operational assessments across major enterprise environments.

The Opening Hook: A Very Bad Tuesday

It’s 2:15 PM on a Tuesday. Your SIEM dashboard lights up like a Christmas tree. Within the span of a few minutes, four separate high-severity alerts hit the queue:

  • Critical: Multiple Brute Force Attempts Detected (Event ID 4625 spiking on a Domain Controller)
  • High: Suspected DNS Tunneling (An endpoint generating thousands of DNS queries to external domains)
  • Critical: Massive Data Exfiltration (A service account transferring 400GB of data outside the traditional network perimeter)
  • High: Privilege Escalation (A standard user account added to the local Administrators group on twenty different machines)

If you are a SOC analyst looking strictly at the telemetry, this looks like a nightmare. It looks like an adversary has achieved a foothold, escalated privileges, established a covert C2 channel via DNS, and is currently walking out the front door with your organization's crown jewels. You declare an incident. You wake up the incident response director. You isolate endpoints.

But an hour later, the dust settles, and you realize the truth.

The "brute force" was just a user who changed their password while Active Directory replication was experiencing a 15-minute delay across sites. The "DNS tunneling" was an Intune software deployment running an update script. The "data exfiltration" was the cloud engineering team migrating an on-prem file server to SharePoint Online. The "privilege escalation" was a helpdesk technician running an approved deployment script via SCCM.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: The tools worked correctly. The detections worked correctly. The problem was missing context.

This article is not about SOC analysts being bad at their jobs. It is about a structural shift in the cybersecurity industry that is making investigations harder, noisier, and less accurate. It is about the Context Crisis.

The Industry Shift Nobody Talks About

To understand the Context Crisis, we have to look at how security professionals are trained today versus fifteen years ago.

Historically, many experienced security analysts came from the trenches of IT operations. They were former Desktop Support technicians, Windows Server administrators, Linux engineers, Network Operations Center (NOC) operators, or Messaging team specialists. Before they ever looked at a SIEM, they had spent years provisioning users, migrating Exchange databases, troubleshooting BGP routes, and untangling messy DHCP scopes.

Because they built the infrastructure, they understood the heartbeat of the network. They understood that Kerberos ticket-granting tickets behave weirdly when a load balancer drops a connection. They knew that service accounts frequently lock themselves out because of orphaned IIS application pools. They understood the chaos of IT operations.

Compare this to the modern pathway into cybersecurity:

  • Intensive security bootcamps.
  • Certifications focused on ethical hacking (CEH) or security fundamentals (Security+).
  • Dedicated SOC analyst training programs.
  • Curriculums heavily focused on the MITRE ATT&CK framework, Sigma rules, and Threat Intelligence.

To be clear: This is not a criticism of these pathways or certifications. They are necessary to meet the incredible demand for security talent, and they do an excellent job of teaching detection concepts, threat actor behaviors, and incident response methodology.

However, these programs often teach detection better than they teach operational behavior. Many analysts today can explain the intricacies of an LSASS memory dump or the exact mechanics of an AS-REP Roasting attack, but they might have a very limited understanding of how Azure AD Connect synchronizes password hashes, or how a DFS replication topology behaves under normal load.

When you know how to find evil, but you do not fully understand what "normal" looks like, every operational anomaly looks like a cyberattack. This is the Context Gap.

The Context Gap: Real-World Examples

The Context Gap occurs when security tooling shows the symptoms, but only infrastructure knowledge reveals the cause. Let’s look at five common scenarios where a lack of operational context turns a trivial IT event into a multi-hour wild goose chase.

Example 1: Password Change vs. Brute Force Alert

The Alert: A user attempts to log in multiple times and fails, generating a massive spike of Event ID 4625 (Failed Logon). The SIEM correlates this into a Brute Force or Password Spraying alert.

The Reality: The user just changed their password. However, in a large, multi-site Active Directory environment, password changes do not instantly propagate to every single domain controller globally. If the user changes their password on `DC01` in New York, and immediately tries to access a file share that authenticates against `DC02` in London before replication has occurred, `DC02` will reject the new password. The user, assuming they typed it wrong, tries five more times rapidly.

Illustrated Detection logic vs. Operational Reality

A standard Sigma rule catches the rapid failures accurately:

# [ILLUSTRATED] Standard Brute Force Sigma Rule
title: High Volume of Failed Logons
detection:
    selection:
        EventID: 4625
    condition: selection | count() > 10 within 1m

But the analyst with infrastructure context doesn't just look for attacker IPs. They run a replication check to see the true cause:

# The Infrastructure Context Check
C:\> repadmin /showrepl
# Output reveals that DC02 has not synced with DC01 for 15 minutes due to a WAN link flap.

An analyst without infrastructure context might assume an attacker is hammering the account. An analyst with context checks the password change timestamp (Event ID 4724) against the failed logon timestamps, understands AD replication delay, and closes the ticket in three minutes as a false positive.

Example 2: Service Account Lockouts

The Alert: A critical service account (`svc_sql_backup`) is repeatedly locking out, triggering alerts for targeted password spraying.

The Reality: The infrastructure team recently rotated the password for the service account to comply with a 90-day policy. However, this service account is used in a scheduled task on an obscure utility server, an application pool in IIS on a legacy web server, and a Windows Service on three different database servers. The team updated the password in four of the five locations, but forgot the legacy IIS server. That server keeps trying to authenticate with the old password, locking the account out.

Security knowledge tells you the account is locked out. Infrastructure knowledge tells you exactly which IIS log or Windows Event log to check to find the originating machine still broadcasting the stale credentials.

Example 3: DNS Tunneling False Positive

The Alert: An endpoint security tool flags a specific workstation for generating an unusually high volume of DNS requests to a series of seemingly random subdomains, indicative of DNS tunneling (a technique used to bypass firewalls and establish C2).

The Reality: The IT department is rolling out a new software package via Microsoft Intune or SCCM. Certain modern cloud applications, particularly those utilizing heavy CDN infrastructures or aggressive background telemetry, can generate massive bursts of DNS queries when initializing, updating, or performing complex synchronizations.

Without knowing the IT deployment schedule or understanding how modern endpoint management tools leverage the network, a SOC analyst might spend hours performing reverse-malware analysis on a benign Intune deployment script.

Example 4: Data Exfiltration Alert

The Alert: Network monitoring detects 500GB of data moving from the internal network to external IP addresses over port 443. The DLP (Data Loss Prevention) system screams "Exfiltration."

The Reality: The enterprise is migrating users' local home drives to Microsoft OneDrive, or the disaster recovery team is performing a scheduled backup replication to a new Azure storage blob. Because the data is moving over encrypted HTTPS (port 443), it looks identical to an attacker exfiltrating data to a cloud drop-site. The difference is entirely contextual.

Example 5: Privilege Escalation Alert

The Alert: A user account is added to the local `Administrators` group on a workstation. The EDR flags this as a classic privilege escalation technique.

The Reality: A helpdesk technician used a temporary elevation tool (like LAPS or a Just-In-Time access management portal) to grant a developer 30 minutes of local admin rights to install a specific debugging tool. Alternatively, it is a managed software deployment that temporarily elevates privileges to run an MSI installer.

The EDR did its job perfectly. But without knowing the organization's IT support workflows, the alert lacks meaning.

The View from Both Sides

The Alert The Security / Threat View The Infrastructure / Operations View
High Volume Failed Logons Brute force attack, password spraying, credential stuffing. AD replication lag, forgotten mobile device syncing old password, stale scheduled task.
Massive Outbound Port 443 Traffic Data exfiltration to attacker-controlled cloud storage. OneDrive migration, off-site backup sync, SharePoint synchronization.
Unusual PowerShell Execution Fileless malware, living-off-the-land (LotL) execution. Automated server maintenance script, RMM tool running a health check.
Local Admin Group Modification Privilege escalation, persistence mechanism. Helpdesk Just-In-Time (JIT) elevation, approved developer access.

The Dashboard Illusion

The Context Gap is exacerbated by what we can call the "Dashboard Illusion."

Ten years ago, investigating an alert often meant logging directly into a server via RDP or SSH, opening the Event Viewer, reading raw log files, and physically tracking the activity through the system. It was slow and painful, but it forced the analyst to interact with the raw infrastructure.

Today, analysts spend 95% of their day interacting with highly abstracted web interfaces:

  • EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) consoles.
  • SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) dashboards.
  • SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response) workflows.
  • XDR platforms that pre-digest and correlate the data.

These tools are incredible feats of engineering, and they are absolutely necessary to handle the scale of modern telemetry. But they create an illusion. They give the analyst the feeling of complete visibility, while actually distancing them from the underlying environment.

When you only look at the world through the lens of a security dashboard, everything looks like a security problem. The dashboard rarely has a button labeled: "Check if the IT guy pushed a bad GPO 10 minutes ago." The dashboard shows you the alert, the MITRE ATT&CK mapping, and the isolated process tree. It highlights the anomalies, but it strips away the mundane operational noise that usually explains those anomalies.

We have increased our visibility exponentially, but our environmental understanding has decreased.

Why This Matters in the AI Era

This brings us to the most critical point. As the industry rushes toward AI-driven security operations, autonomous agents, and LLM-powered triage, the Context Gap is about to become a massive liability.

⚠️ Editorial Analysis: The Limits of AI Triage

There is a pervasive belief that AI will soon handle all Tier 1 SOC duties, completely eliminating the false positive problem. This is a dangerous oversimplification. AI is an amplifier of context, not a replacement for it.

Generative AI and Large Language Models are phenomenal at certain tasks. They can summarize a 50-page incident report in seconds. They can correlate telemetry across disparate log sources. They can write beautiful, grammatically perfect investigation notes. They can even suggest the correct KQL or Splunk query to run next.

But AI currently struggles profoundly when it lacks environmental context. An AI agent analyzing a SIEM alert cannot automatically know:

  • Why the accounting department relies on a 15-year-old legacy application that performs a bizarre SMB directory traversal every Friday at 4 PM.
  • Why the HR onboarding process utilizes a custom, undocumented PowerShell script that triggers three different heuristic alarms.
  • Why a specific service account is allowed to bypass standard authentication policies because the vendor refuses to update their API.
  • The informal agreements between the IT director and the development team regarding production server access.

AI models are trained on the internet. They know what a "textbook" cyberattack looks like. But enterprise networks are not textbooks. They are messy, duct-taped conglomerates of technical debt, legacy systems, workaround scripts, and human business logic.

If an AI is fed an alert without the operational context of the business, it will confidently hallucinate an attack narrative based on the technical symptoms. It will tell you that the Intune deployment is a beaconing C2 channel. It will tell you the AD replication delay is a brute force campaign.

Therefore, the future security analyst will not need less infrastructure knowledge because of AI. They will need more. The human analyst's primary job will shift from manually querying logs to providing the operational context and business logic that the AI lacks. They will be the bridge between the AI's technical analysis and the reality of the enterprise environment.

Closing the Context Gap

If the cybersecurity industry wants to reduce alert fatigue, shorten investigation times, and stop treating normal IT operations as critical incidents, we have to close the Context Gap. We have to reconnect security knowledge with operational reality.

Here are practical steps organizations can take to bridge this divide:

1. SOC Rotations into Infrastructure Teams

Do not let your analysts live entirely inside the SIEM. Mandate short rotations (even just a few days a quarter) where SOC analysts shadow the Helpdesk, the Network Operations Center, or the Systems Administration team. Let them see how users actually request access, how servers are patched, and how backups are configured. Understanding the friction of IT operations makes you infinitely better at securing them.

2. Cross-Pollinate Engineering Teams

Detection engineers should regularly sit with Active Directory architects and Cloud Identity engineers. If you are writing a Sigma rule to detect Kerberoasting, you should sit with the person who actually configures the Service Principal Names (SPNs) in your environment. You cannot effectively write detections for systems you do not understand.

3. Context-Driven Purple Teaming

Standard purple team exercises involve the Red Team attacking and the Blue Team defending. Expand this. Bring the IT Operations and Infrastructure teams into the exercise. When the Red Team simulates an attack, ask the IT team: "Does this look like any normal administrative activity we do?" Their answers will be illuminating and will immediately highlight where your detections need to be tuned.

4. Update Security Training Curriculums

Security bootcamps and enterprise training programs need to reintegrate IT fundamentals. Before teaching an analyst how to detect a Golden Ticket attack, ensure they can explain the standard Kerberos authentication flow. Before teaching them about DNS tunneling, ensure they understand how split-brain DNS works in a corporate environment. The foundation must be solid.

Conclusion

Cybersecurity is ultimately the practice of protecting systems. It is logically impossible to effectively protect—or investigate—a system that you do not fundamentally understand.

We have built an incredible ecosystem of tools capable of detecting the faintest whisper of malicious activity. But tools only provide data. Context provides meaning. Without operational context, security alerts are just noise, and investigations are just exercises in chasing ghosts.

The best analysts are not always the ones who know the most MITRE techniques, threat actors, or security products. They are often the people who understand how enterprise systems behave on a normal Tuesday afternoon.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What is the Context Gap in cybersecurity?

The Context Gap refers to the growing disconnect between a security analyst's knowledge of threat frameworks and detection tools versus their understanding of how enterprise infrastructure and operational systems actually behave on a day-to-day basis.

Why do SIEMs and EDRs generate so many false positives from normal IT operations?

Security tools are designed to detect behaviors that match attacker techniques, such as rapid authentications, high-volume DNS requests, or massive file transfers. Normal IT operations—like SCCM software deployments, Intune syncs, or backup replication—often mirror these exact behaviors, triggering alerts that lack operational context.

How can SOC analysts learn infrastructure context?

Analysts can close the context gap by participating in SOC rotations with infrastructure teams, shadowing Active Directory or network engineering teams, engaging in purple team exercises that involve IT operations, and integrating systems administration fundamentals into their ongoing training.

Will AI solve the problem of false positives in cybersecurity?

While AI can summarize alerts, correlate telemetry, and draft investigation notes rapidly, it struggles without environmental context. AI cannot automatically know why a legacy application behaves strangely or why a custom authentication workflow exists. Therefore, human analysts need more infrastructure understanding, not less, to guide AI effectively.
