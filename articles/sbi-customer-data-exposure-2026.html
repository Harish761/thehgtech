The SBI Data Exposure: What 3,897 PII Records on the Dark Web Reveal About Data Security and Breach Response

SBI disclosed that 3,897 customer records with PII were found in ~375,000 records on the dark web. Analysis of what is confirmed, what is alleged, what the counts mean, and what defenders should investigate.

1. Detailed Analysis
CONFIRMED DATA LOSS · SOURCE UNDETERMINED

3,897 customer records with PII identified in ~375,000 records examined (preliminary, per SBI)

Status last checked: 22 October 2026, 00:05 IST · No attack vector, attacker, or affected system has been named by SBI

Key Takeaways

  • What SBI confirmed: In a Regulation 30 filing dated 21 October 2026, SBI said it "has experienced an incident involving the loss of certain data" and that 3,897 customer records containing PII were identified during preliminary analysis of approximately 375,000 records on the dark web.
  • What the data reportedly contains: Registered mobile numbers, dates of birth, names, gender, email addresses, residential addresses, branch and CIF identifiers, lead IDs, and metadata associated with loan/lead records. SBI itself uses the word "reportedly".
  • What is not established: How the data left SBI's control, whether any SBI system was breached, whether a vendor or subsidiary was involved, how many unique customers are affected, and what the remaining ~371,000 records contain.
  • What is alleged, not confirmed: A threat actor using the name "Siriusim" claimed on BreachForums to have released 375,000 CSV rows and 2,722 PDF documents, including loan applications and account-opening forms. SBI's filing does not name the actor, the forum, or the PDFs.
  • Realistic risk: The listed fields are well suited to targeted impersonation of the bank. No fraud, fund loss, or credential compromise has been reported.
What Security Teams Should Do Now
  • Fraud and contact-centre teams (any Indian bank or NBFC): brief agents that callers may hold accurate customer names, dates of birth, addresses, branch and CIF details. Possession of these details must not be treated as proof of identity.
  • SOC teams: review access and export activity on CRM, lead-management, and loan-origination platforms, including vendor and direct-selling-agent accounts, for bulk reads and unusual export volumes.
  • Data-protection teams: inventory where internal customer identifiers (such as CIF numbers) and lead data are replicated outside core systems, and who can access them.
  • Incident responders handling a similar claim: obtain and hash the published dataset under legal guidance, match it against internal records without redistributing PII, and fingerprint the schema to identify the source system before drawing conclusions.

1. Executive Summary

On 21 October 2026, State Bank of India (SBI), India's largest commercial bank, told the BSE and NSE that it had "experienced an incident involving the loss of certain data." According to the filing, the bank received alerts on 16 October 2026 from its Threat Intelligence Service Provider, the Defence Cyber Agency, and CERT-In about the suspected exposure of its customer data on the dark web. A preliminary analysis of approximately 375,000 records identified 3,897 customer records containing personally identifiable information (PII).

The filing is careful in its language, and the analysis should be too. SBI states that a detailed investigation is required "to establish the authenticity and completeness of the exposed data, determine the potential source and extent of the exposure, and assess the associated risks to customers and the Bank." It refers to "the alleged compromise" and has engaged an independent CERT-In-empanelled cybersecurity agency to investigate. It does not name an attacker, a forum, a compromised system, a vendor, or an attack technique.

In practical terms, the incident is best described as a confirmed loss of a limited set of customer PII whose origin is still undetermined. It is not, on current evidence, a confirmed compromise of SBI's core banking platform, customer credentials, or funds. The SBI filing says the incident has no material impact on the bank's core business operations, financial performance, or business continuity. As The Hacker News pointed out, that statement covers the bank's business, not its customers.

The exposed fields matter because they are exactly what a fraudster needs to sound like a bank employee: name, mobile number, date of birth, address, home branch, and an internal customer identifier. That is the most important practical takeaway for defenders, and it does not depend on how the investigation into the source turns out.

2. What Happened

The threat actor claim (16–17 October)

On 16 October 2026, the threat-monitoring account CyberPulse56 flagged a claim on X that a threat actor using the name "Siriusim" had leaked customer documents and personal data linked to SBI. As reported by The Hacker News and The Free Press Journal, the claim described 375,000 rows in CSV format and 2,722 PDF documents, said to include loan applications and supporting documents, KYC records, account-opening forms, and addresses. The listing was posted on BreachForums.

The Free Press Journal examined four sample records published with the listing and reported that the phone numbers were valid, and that a WhatsApp profile picture on one number matched the photograph on that person's KYC document, which the actor had also posted. Its report was headlined as concerning SBI and its subsidiaries, and noted that SBI had not responded at the time of publication. As The Hacker News observed, a check of four records cannot establish that the whole dataset is genuine, nor whether the data came from SBI itself, a subsidiary, or a third party that processes banking applications.

SBI's disclosure (21 October)

Five days after the alerts, SBI filed its disclosure under Regulation 30 of the SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015. The filing, signed by the bank's DGM (Compliance & Company Secretary), makes the following points:

  • Alerts were received on 16 October 2026 from three parties: SBI's Threat Intelligence Service Provider, the Defence Cyber Agency, and CERT-In.
  • Approximately 375,000 records "available on the dark web" were analysed; 3,897 customer records containing PII were identified.
  • The exposed information "reportedly included" registered mobile numbers, dates of birth, names, gender, email addresses, residential addresses, branch and CIF identifiers, lead IDs, and metadata associated with loan/lead records "and other related files".
  • SBI activated its cyber incident response and containment protocols and engaged an independent CERT-In-empanelled agency.
  • Remedial and preventive measures are "being implemented as required".

Importantly, nothing in the filing explicitly links SBI's analysis to the Siriusim listing. The match between the two figures (about 375,000) is strong circumstantial evidence that they describe the same dataset, but it is reporters and the Internet Freedom Foundation, not SBI, who have drawn that link. The filing does not mention the 2,722 PDF documents.

A note on headline language

Some coverage has described the incident as a "cyberattack" or a "hack". SBI's filing uses neither word. It describes a "loss of certain data" and an "alleged compromise". Data can reach the dark web through an external intrusion, but also through a vendor, an intermediary such as a direct selling agent, a misconfigured storage location, or an insider. Until the investigation reports, all of these remain possibilities.

4. What the Record Counts Actually Mean

Two figures dominate the coverage, and they are easy to misread. Neither is a count of affected people.

Interpreting the published figures
Figure What it measures What it does not establish
~375,000 Records "available on the dark web" that SBI examined in its preliminary analysis. The same figure appears in the actor's claim of 375,000 CSV rows. That all are SBI data, that all are customer records, or that they are unique. Rows may include duplicates, lead entries for non-customers, or non-personal records.
3,897 Records identified as "customer records containing PII" in the preliminary analysis. That is about 1.04% of the records examined (3,897 ÷ 375,000). The number of unique customers, compromised accounts, or confirmed victims. SBI has not said whether the 3,897 records belong to 3,897 different people.
2,722 PDF documents claimed by the threat actor. Not mentioned in SBI's filing. Whether SBI examined them, and what they contain, is unknown.
~371,100 The remainder of the examined set (375,000 − 3,897 = 371,103) that was not identified as customer PII in the preliminary pass. That these records are harmless. SBI has not said what they contain. The count is explicitly preliminary.

Several explanations could fit the gap between 375,000 and 3,897, and the evidence does not yet favour any of them. The larger set may be mostly lead records, which by definition can relate to prospects who never became customers. It may contain operational or metadata rows with no personal fields. It may include records SBI could not match to its own customers, which would be consistent with data originating from a subsidiary or intermediary. Or the preliminary analysis may simply be incomplete. The filing describes the figure as preliminary, and readers should expect it to change.

Why "records" are not "people"

In lead- and loan-management systems, one person commonly appears in several rows: one per application, product enquiry, co-applicant role, or status update. Equally, one row can describe more than one person (a joint applicant or guarantor). Until a dataset is de-duplicated against a master customer key, a record count is an upper or lower bound on affected individuals, not a measure of them.

7. Realistic Threat Scenarios

The scenarios below are possibilities derived from the listed fields. None has been reported as occurring in connection with this incident. They are included because they define what defenders and customer-facing teams should prepare for.

7.1 Bank-impersonation calls (vishing)

A caller recites the customer's name, date of birth, address, home branch, and CIF number, then claims there is a problem with a loan or KYC record that needs "verification". The goal is to obtain an OTP, UPI PIN, card details, or to persuade the customer to install a remote-access app. The exposed fields do not let an attacker into an account. They make the customer more likely to help. As the IFF put it, in a statement reported by The Hindu, affected customers are "the most likely to receive calls from persons posing as SBI officials who can recite their account details back to them."

7.2 Loan-themed fraud

Because the data reportedly includes loan and lead metadata, the obvious lure is a loan: a "pre-approved" offer requiring an upfront processing fee, a fake disbursement that needs "confirmation", or a request to re-submit documents through a lookalike portal. Customers with a genuine pending application are especially vulnerable, because the lure matches what they expect to hear.

7.3 Targeted phishing and smishing

Email addresses and mobile numbers paired with real context support phishing that links to credential-harvesting pages imitating SBI's internet banking or YONO login. Accurate personal detail in the message reduces the usual warning signs.

7.4 Weakened knowledge-based verification

Any organisation, not only SBI, that verifies callers using name, date of birth, address, and mobile number should treat those factors as weaker for the affected population. This matters for telecom providers' SIM-replacement processes and for other financial institutions.

7.5 Identity fraud (conditional)

Only if the claimed KYC documents and account-opening forms are genuine and contain identity-document images, the risk would extend to synthetic-identity and fraudulent account-opening attempts. That condition is unconfirmed.

What cannot be concluded from the evidence
  • That SBI's core banking system, internet banking, or YONO was compromised.
  • That passwords, OTPs, card data, account numbers, or balances were exposed.
  • That 3,897, or 375,000, people are affected.
  • That the data came from SBI's own systems rather than a subsidiary, vendor, or intermediary.
  • Any specific attack technique, malware family, or threat-actor capability.

On current evidence, a blanket password reset for all SBI customers has no specific justification. The listed data contains no credentials. Vigilance against impersonation is the proportionate response.

8. Technical Investigation: What Defenders Should Examine

This section describes how an investigation into an exposure like this is typically scoped. It does not describe SBI's environment, which has not been disclosed. It applies to any organisation that finds its customer data offered on a criminal forum.

8.1 Establish what the dataset is before asking how it left

The fastest route to the source is usually the data itself. Under legal guidance and with evidence handling in place, responders should:

  1. Acquire and preserve the dataset as published, with cryptographic hashes and acquisition notes (URL, time, method), so that later analysis can be reproduced.
  2. Fingerprint the schema. Column names, column order, internal codes, date formats, enumerations, and identifier formats (lead ID patterns, branch code formats) often match exactly one internal system or export template.
  3. Establish the time window. The latest record creation or update timestamp gives an earliest possible extraction date. Records missing a known recent change give a latest possible one.
  4. Match against the system of record to measure authenticity: what fraction of rows correspond to real customers, real leads, or nothing at all. Use keyed hashes rather than raw PII where possible (see Section 9.1).
  5. De-duplicate on a stable customer key to convert "records" into "affected individuals", and separate customers from non-customer leads.
  6. Check for distribution artefacts. Fields only present in a vendor's copy, a specific report, or a particular channel partner's export narrow the source considerably.

8.2 Candidate sources to evaluate

Data described as lead IDs and loan/lead metadata typically lives in CRM, lead-management, and loan-origination platforms, and in the systems of the partners that feed them. Without assuming any of these is the source in SBI's case, a defensible investigation would cover:

Investigation scope: candidate sources, telemetry, and key questions
Candidate source Telemetry to collect Investigation questions
CRM / lead-management platformApplication audit logs, report and export logs, API access logs, admin configuration changesWho ran bulk reads or exports matching the dataset's schema and time window? Were any reports newly created or modified?

8.3 Evidence preservation

  • Extend log retention immediately on all candidate systems. Application audit logs in SaaS platforms often have short default retention.
  • Snapshot relevant configurations (sharing rules, report definitions, API client registrations) before remediation changes them.
  • Request preservation of logs from third parties in writing, referencing contractual audit rights.
  • Maintain a chain of custody for the acquired dataset and restrict access to it, because it contains live PII.

9. Detection and Response Guidance

9.1 Matching a leaked dataset without spreading PII

Analysts frequently need to compare a leaked file against internal records, sometimes with external parties involved. Comparing keyed hashes (HMAC) of normalised identifiers rather than raw values limits further exposure. The following Python uses only the standard library. It is an illustrative example: column names such as mobile are placeholders that must be mapped to the actual schema, and the key must be generated and held by the data owner.

PYTHON PYTHON Artifact 
# Illustrative: keyed-hash matching of a leaked CSV against an internal extract.
# Prerequisites: Python 3.8+, both files available locally under legal authorisation,
# and a secret key held by the data owner (never shared with third parties).
import csv, hmac, hashlib, re

KEY = b"replace-with-a-32-byte-secret-from-your-kms"

def norm_mobile(value: str) -> str:
    digits = re.sub(r"\D", "", value or "")
    return digits[-10:]  # Indian mobile numbers: compare on the last 10 digits

def token(value: str) -> str:
    return hmac.new(KEY, value.encode(), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()

def tokens(path: str, column: str) -> set:
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        return {token(norm_mobile(row[column])) for row in csv.DictReader(f)
                if norm_mobile(row.get(column, ""))}

leaked = tokens("leaked.csv", "mobile")            # placeholder column name
internal = tokens("internal_extract.csv", "mobile")  # placeholder column name
print(f"unique leaked identifiers: {len(leaked)}")
print(f"matched to internal records: {len(leaked & internal)}")

The ratio of matched to unique identifiers is a direct, reportable measure of authenticity, and the count of unique matches is a better estimate of affected individuals than a raw row count.

9.2 Monitoring for bulk access to customer data (template)

Every CRM and loan platform logs differently, so the following is pseudocode, not a working query. Field names in angle brackets must be replaced with those in your own audit log schema. The logic flags identities whose volume of customer-record reads or exports is far above their own baseline.

CODE CODE Artifact 
// TEMPLATE / PSEUDOCODE: adapt field and table names to your audit log schema
source  = <crm_or_los_audit_log>
window  = last 1 day
baseline_window = previous 30 days

events  = source where <action> in ("export", "report_run", "bulk_api_read", "download")
                    and <object_type> in ("customer", "lead", "loan_application")

today    = events in window          group by <actor_id> sum(<record_count>) as today_rows
baseline = events in baseline_window group by <actor_id> daily p95(<record_count>) as p95_rows

alert where today_rows > max(5 * p95_rows, <absolute_floor>)
       or (<actor_type> == "service_account" and <source_ip> not in <approved_ranges>)
       or (<actor_org> is third_party and today_rows > <partner_threshold>)

Practical notes for implementing this logic:

  • Many SaaS CRMs only record report exports or API events when an additional event-monitoring or audit licence is enabled. Confirm the events exist before relying on the detection.
  • Per-identity baselines work better than global thresholds, because legitimate bulk users (marketing, analytics, partner integrations) will otherwise dominate the alerts.
  • Third-party and service-account access deserves separate, tighter thresholds.

9.3 DLP and egress monitoring ideas

  • Create DLP fingerprints or exact-data-match profiles for internal identifier formats (CIF numbers, lead IDs) combined with mobile numbers. A file containing many of both leaving the network is a high-fidelity signal.
  • Seed canary (honeytoken) customer and lead records in CRM and partner feeds, with monitored contact details. Contact attempts to a canary identify both a leak and the feed it came from.
  • Alert on uploads above a size threshold from data-holding systems to file-sharing, paste, or messaging services.

9.4 Customer-facing response

  • Brief contact-centre and branch staff that knowledge of DOB, address, branch, and CIF is not proof of identity for the affected population.
  • Watch for spikes in SIM-swap requests, mobile-number change requests, and new-device registrations among affected customers, and apply step-up verification.
  • Send customers specific, actionable advice: the bank will not ask for OTPs or PINs; report suspicious contacts to report.phishing@sbi.co.in (per SBI's general guidance as reported by THN) or to India's cybercrime helpline, 1930.

10. Data Protection and Security Control Lessons

The investigation has not identified which control, if any, failed at SBI, and this section does not suggest otherwise. These are the controls that most directly reduce the likelihood and impact of this class of exposure, organised by function.

Controls by function for lead and loan data
Function Controls
PreventionData minimisation in lead and partner feeds (do partners need DOB and CIF?); field-level masking of internal identifiers; least-privilege roles and export restrictions; contractual and technical controls on DSAs and vendors; retention limits that purge stale leads.
DetectionAudit logging of reads and exports (not only writes); per-identity volume baselines; exact-data-match DLP; canary records; external threat-intelligence monitoring of criminal forums. In this case, external parties detected the exposure first.
ContainmentAbility to revoke partner and service-account access quickly; pre-approved playbooks for disabling bulk export; takedown requests where feasible.
RecoveryCustomer notification and support processes; step-up verification for affected customers; post-incident review of data flows; publication of findings.

The notification gap

The incident highlights a structural issue in Indian breach response that is separate from technical security. According to the IFF statement reported by The Hindu, Section 8(6) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and Rule 7 of the DPDP Rules, 2025, would require notification to the Data Protection Board and to each affected individual. The IFF says these provisions take effect only on 13 May 2027. It also says the obligations in force today, under the CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022 and the RBI's cyber security framework for banks, are owed to regulators rather than to customers. This is the IFF's legal characterisation, and we have not independently assessed it. If it is accurate, customer notification in this case depends on the bank's discretion rather than a legal requirement.

Organisations do not need to wait for that deadline. Affected people can only guard against targeted impersonation if they know they are targets.

12. Conclusion

On the evidence available as of 22 October 2026, SBI has confirmed a loss of customer data, sized at 3,897 PII-bearing records in a preliminary review of roughly 375,000. The listed fields are contact and profile data, internal identifiers, and loan/lead metadata. No credentials or financial data are listed. The source, the attack vector if there was one, the number of unique people affected, and the contents of the rest of the dataset and the claimed PDFs remain unknown. SBI reports no impact on its operations, and no fraud has been reported.

Two lessons apply regardless of how the investigation concludes. First, the bank learned of the exposure from external parties after the data was already available. That is an argument for monitoring reads and exports of customer data, including by partners, with the same rigour applied to logins. Second, the most likely harm here is social engineering, which customers can only defend against if they are told.

The questions that will determine how serious this incident is:

  • Did the data come from SBI, a subsidiary, a vendor, or an intermediary?
  • How many unique individuals are affected, and what do the remaining records contain?
  • Were the 2,722 claimed PDFs examined, and do they contain identity documents?
  • Will affected customers be notified, and will the investigation's findings be published?

TheHGTech will update this analysis if SBI, a regulator, or the investigating agency publishes new findings.
